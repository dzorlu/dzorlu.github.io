Tinkerings

A map of weight edits

State on 14 November 2026, 19:10 · live RL run · step through the trajectories · changelog · code

A language model can improve a smaller model by reading its weights and activations and overwriting a few values, with no gradients [1] [2]. This page lists every edit our editor models have tried on one target, Qwen3.5-0.8B, sorted by what kind of change it is and what it was aimed at.

The target fails at math in one way. It writes "Wait..." and rechecks its work until it runs out of tokens, in about 95% of answers. Each edit below tries to stop that. Scores are the share of correct final answers.

How to diagnose

A diagnosis finds the units that make the model write the wrong next token. One fact does the work. A token's logit is a dot product, and the residual stream is a sum. So the logit splits into one part per layer, and each layer part splits into one contribution per unit.

A small network

Two layers, a residual stream of 2 numbers instead of 1024, 3 MLP units per layer instead of 3584, and 3 tokens. Each layer reads the stream and computes its activations x. Its output matrix W has one row per unit, and the layer writes m = x @ W back into the stream. The unembedding matrix U has one column per token and turns the final vector into one logit per token.

Where a logit comes from

Take one layer. Its output matrix W has one row per unit. The column of U for "Wait" is u. Two multiplications give every weight's contribution to the "Wait" logit:

  1. Multiply each column of W by the matching entry of u.
  2. Multiply each row by that unit's activation x[j].

Hover a cell to see its contribution, or a row's total to see one unit's.

A row's total is that unit's contribution. All the cells together are the layer's. Doing this for every layer of the real model, 24 matrices of [3584 × 1024], shows which layer and which unit to delete or edit. This is called direct logit attribution [3] [4].

What an edit changes

An edit changes s = W @ u, so it changes every contribution that uses it. Zeroing one row removes one unit's contribution. Removing the direction u from every row makes s zero for all units, so the layer can no longer change the "Wait" logit at all.

The steps on the real model

  1. Pick the token at fault. "Wait" opens 7.5 lines per 1,000 tokens in answers cut off at the token limit, and 1.4 in answers that finish. u is its column of U.
  2. Record activations. Send several looping answers back through the model in one teacher-forced pass. For each layer you get x_L at every position: shape [tokens, 3584].
  3. Choose the positions. The positions just before each "Wait".
  4. Compute contributions, every layer. s_L = W_L @ u, then x_L[positions] * s_L, summed over the positions: a table of [24 layers, 3584 units].
  5. Rank. Row sums give each layer's part; the largest entries are the candidate units.
  6. Check when they fire. Plot the top unit's activation over all positions to see what it responds to.
  7. Test. Edit the unit's row, generate with original and edited weights on the same problems, and try several strengths.
  8. Validate. Run the chosen edit on problems that played no part in choosing it.

A contribution counts only what a unit writes straight into the final vector, not what later layers do with it. That is why step 7 is never skipped. A second method finds a direction without picking a token: average the activations where the model loops, and subtract the average where it does not [6] [7].

Two diagnoses from our runs

Inkling 2 Small, in its first search, took one of Qwen's own looping answers, 719 tokens long, and ran it back through Qwen. It read the MLP activations of layers 12 to 23. At every position it computed each layer's MLP part of the logit for two tokens: "</think>", the token that ends the reasoning, and "Wait". Figure C shows each layer's part summed over the 719 positions.

Inkling concluded three things. The MLPs of layers 15 to 21 lower the logit of "</think>". The MLP of layer 22 raises the logit of "Wait" by the most, +210. Only layer 23 raises the logit of "</think>".

It then looked for single units with large contributions. It found many small ones and no dominant one. So it edited a direction instead of units. It set d to U[:, </think>] scaled to length 1, and changed the output matrix of the MLPs of layers 20 to 23 by W + α·d·(dᵀW), kind 5 under How to update below. This raises what those MLPs write along d. It tried α = 0.5, 0.7, 0.85 and 1.0. At α = 0.85, test accuracy went from 6.6% to 20.2%.

Claude Opus 5.5 did the same split for "Wait", at the positions just before a "Wait". It found one dominant unit: unit 998 of the layer-16 MLP. Its contribution was about +2 to +3, out of a "Wait" logit of about 17 to 18. The unit has a large negative activation right after a result is computed, the moment before a recheck. A negative activation and a positive contribution mean its row of W points away from u. Zeroing that row raised train accuracy from 7.0% to 14.5%. Negating it gave 17.6%.

How to update

Every edit so far changes an output matrix: the matrix that writes an MLP's or an attention layer's result into the residual stream. Nothing else has been edited. Column j of an MLP output matrix is what unit j adds. An edit either changes one or a few columns, so what single units write, or adds a rank-one term a·bᵀ that touches every column [5], so what the whole matrix writes along one direction. Both kinds appear in the table below: Opus's and Inkling's largest gains came from rank-one terms.

The figure below draws W as 6 rows by 10 columns, before and after the edit. Rows are residual dimensions and columns are MLP units. positive weight, negative weight, zero. A stronger colour is a larger weight. marks the cells the edit changes. The other cells are faded.

Kinds 1 to 4 change one column of an MLP output matrix. The other columns stay the same. Kinds 5 to 7 are rank-one changes. They can touch every entry of the matrix. Cell (i, j) changes by the product of entry i of the vertical vector and entry j of the horizontal vector.

Each edit is aimed at one of two behaviours:

  • Stop the loop. Lower the score of the token "Wait".
  • Close the reasoning. Raise the score of the token that ends the thinking section, so the model moves on to an answer.

Every edit tried so far

Problems come in four sets. Train: MATH and GSM8K training problems the editor uses during its search to diagnose and to choose between edits. Validation: train problems it set aside to check its final edit, never used for a choice. Test: MATH-500, run once after the search on the submitted edit; the editor never sees it. Reward set: 128 hidden MATH training problems that score each RL rollout. All numbers are accuracy changes in points, edited minus original, on the same problems.

What the map shows

  • The best edit does both things. Opus measured each half alone at about 16 to 18% and the two together at 33 to 36%. Stopping "Wait" does little if the model still never closes its reasoning, and the reverse.
  • One unit keeps turning up. Column 998 of the layer-16 MLP was found by Opus and, separately, by two of the four RL rollouts. The same unit is edited in two checkpoints of arXiv 2611.09382.
  • Pushing too hard is the common failure. Raise the score of the end-of-thinking token too much and the model stops reasoning. Opus, Inkling and an RL rollout each ran into it, and the ones with budget for a strength sweep backed off.
  • One well-chosen unit beat eleven. Opus's single flipped unit was worth more than the eleven units Inkling zeroed in its second search.
  • The largest gains spread a direction over every layer. In the hero run's first step the two best rollouts, +33.6 and +27.3, both removed the "Wait" direction and added the end-of-thinking direction in the output matrices of all 24 layers. Editing a few columns gave +1 to +19.
  • All of this fixes one failure. The edits remove an obstacle, the loop. None of them adds an ability the model lacked.
