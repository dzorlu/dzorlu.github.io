A map of weight edits

State on 14 November 2026 · step through the trajectories · changelog · code

A language model can improve a smaller model by reading its weights and activations and overwriting a few values, with no gradients [1] [2]. This page lists every edit our editor models have tried on one target, Qwen3.5-0.8B, sorted by what kind of change it is and what it was aimed at.

The target fails at math in one way. It writes "Wait..." and rechecks its work until it runs out of tokens, in about 95% of answers. Each edit below tries to stop that. Scores are the share of correct final answers.

How to update

Every edit so far changes an output matrix: the matrix that writes an MLP's or an attention layer's result into the residual stream. Nothing else has been edited. Column j of an MLP output matrix is what unit j adds. An edit either changes one or a few columns, so what single units write, or adds a rank-one term a·bᵀ that touches every column [5], so what the whole matrix writes along one direction. Both kinds appear in the table below: Opus's and Inkling's largest gains came from rank-one terms.

The figure below draws W as 6 rows by 10 columns, before and after the edit. Rows are residual dimensions and columns are MLP units. positive weight, negative weight, zero. A stronger colour is a larger weight. marks the cells the edit changes. The other cells are faded.

Kinds 1 to 4 change one column of an MLP output matrix. The other columns stay the same. Kinds 5 to 7 are rank-one changes. They can touch every entry of the matrix. Cell (i, j) changes by the product of entry i of the vertical vector and entry j of the horizontal vector.

Each edit is aimed at one of two behaviours:

  • Stop the loop. Lower the score of the token "Wait".
  • Close the reasoning. Raise the score of the token that ends the thinking section, so the model moves on to an answer.

How to diagnose

A diagnosis finds the units that make the model write the wrong next token. It takes one number, the score of that token, and splits it into one share per layer, then one share per unit.

The score of a token

After the last layer, the model holds one vector h of 1024 numbers for the current position. Each candidate next token t has an output vector r_t: its row of the embedding matrix, times the gain of the final norm. The score of t is one number:

score(t) = dot(r_t, h) / rms(h)

The token with the highest score is the most likely next token.

h is a sum. It starts as the token's embedding, and every layer adds its output to it. So the score splits into one share per layer:

share_L = dot(r_t, output of layer L) / rms(h)

The shares add up to the score. Figure A works this out with 3 numbers in place of 1024.

The share of one unit

The output of an MLP layer is W @ x: the sum over units j of activation x[j] times column W[:, j]. So a layer's share splits again, into one share per unit:

share_L,j = x[j] * dot(r_t, W[:, j]) / rms(h)

The first factor is the unit's activation. The second, dot(r_t, W[:, j]), is large when the column points the same way as r_t, zero when it is at a right angle to r_t, and negative when it points the opposite way. A unit's share is large only when both factors are large. A positive share raises the score of t, so t becomes more likely. A negative share lowers it.

This split is called direct logit attribution [3] [4]. It counts what a unit adds to h directly. It ignores how that addition changes what later layers write. So it gives candidates, and an edit followed by generation confirms them.

Two diagnoses from our runs

Inkling 2 Small, in its first search, took one of Qwen's own looping answers, 719 tokens long, and ran it back through Qwen. It read the MLP activations of layers 12 to 23. At every position it computed each layer's MLP share for two tokens: "</think>", the token that ends the reasoning, and "Wait". Figure C shows each layer's share summed over the 719 positions.

Inkling concluded three things. The MLPs of layers 15 to 21 lower the score of "</think>". The MLP of layer 22 raises the score of "Wait" by the most, +210. Only layer 23 raises the score of "</think>".

It then looked for single units with large shares. It found many small ones and no dominant one. So it edited a direction instead of units. It set d to r_</think> scaled to length 1, and changed the output matrix of the MLPs of layers 20 to 23 by W + α·d·(dᵀW), kind 5 in the figure above. This raises what those MLPs write along d. It tried α = 0.5, 0.7, 0.85 and 1.0. At α = 0.85, test accuracy went from 6.6% to 20.2%.

Claude Opus 5.5 did the same split for "Wait", at the positions just before a "Wait". It found one dominant unit: unit 998 of the layer-16 MLP. Its share was about +2 to +3, out of a "Wait" score of about 17 to 18. The unit has a large negative activation right after a result is computed, the moment before a recheck. A negative activation and a positive share mean its column points away from r_Wait. Zeroing column 998 raised train accuracy from 7.0% to 14.5%. Negating it gave 17.6%.

The steps

  1. Read the failing answers and pick the token at fault. "Wait" opens 7.5 lines per 1,000 tokens in answers cut off at the token limit, and 1.4 in answers that finish.
  2. Run a failing answer back through the model in one teacher-forced pass, and record the activations.
  3. Split the score of that token by layer, then by unit, at the positions where it matters.
  4. Check when the units with the largest shares are active. Unit 998 is active right after a result is computed.
  5. Edit those units, or a direction, and generate. Try several strengths. A steady rise with strength shows the edit is the cause, and the sweep shows where it starts to do harm.
  6. Check the chosen edit on validation problems, which played no part in choosing it.

A second method finds a direction without picking a token. Average the activations at positions where the model loops, and subtract the average at positions where it does not. The difference is a direction for looping [6] [7]. One RL rollout removed such a direction from the attention output matrix of layer 23, kind 6 in the figure above.

Every edit tried so far

Problems come in four sets. Train: MATH and GSM8K training problems the editor uses during its search to diagnose and to choose between edits. Validation: train problems it set aside to check its final edit, never used for a choice. Test: MATH-500, run once after the search on the submitted edit; the editor never sees it. Reward set: 128 hidden MATH training problems that score each RL rollout. All numbers are accuracy changes in points, edited minus original, on the same problems.

What the map shows

  • The best edit does both things. Opus measured each half alone at about 16 to 18% and the two together at 33 to 36%. Stopping "Wait" does little if the model still never closes its reasoning, and the reverse.
  • One unit keeps turning up. Column 998 of the layer-16 MLP was found by Opus and, separately, by two of the four RL rollouts. The same unit is edited in two checkpoints of arXiv 2611.09382.
  • Pushing too hard is the common failure. Strengthen the end-of-thinking signal too much and the model stops reasoning. Opus, Inkling and an RL rollout each ran into it, and the ones with budget for a strength sweep backed off.
  • One well-chosen unit beat eleven. Opus's single flipped unit was worth more than the eleven units Inkling zeroed in its second search.
  • All of this fixes one failure. The edits remove an obstacle, the loop. None of them adds an ability the model lacked.
